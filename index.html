Overview

In this homework I built a software rasterizer that renders a simplified subset of SVG. It starts from the most basic operation, deciding which pixels a single-color triangle covers using three edge functions over the triangle's bounding box, and builds up from there. Supersampling stores an \(n \times n\) grid of samples per pixel in a larger sample buffer and averages them down at the end of the frame, which turns hard stair-stepped edges into smooth ones and keeps sub-pixel slivers from breaking apart. Translate, scale, and rotate matrices in homogeneous coordinates let nested SVG groups compose into a posable figure. Barycentric coordinates, which fall straight out of the edge functions, interpolate per-vertex colors and then per-vertex texture coordinates across a triangle. Finally, texture mapping samples an image with nearest or bilinear pixel filtering, and mipmap level sampling picks the right resolution of the texture from the screen-space rate of change of \((u, v)\), which removes the moiré that appears when a detailed texture is minified.

The thing I found most interesting is how much of the pipeline reuses one idea. The edge function that answers "is this sample inside?" also gives the barycentric weights, and those same weights interpolate colors, texture coordinates, and even the coordinates at neighboring pixels that determine the mip level. I also came away with a much more concrete feel for aliasing. Supersampling, bilinear filtering, and mipmapping all look like different tricks, but each one is a way of averaging over the area a pixel really covers instead of trusting a single point: supersampling does it in screen space, bilinear does it for magnified textures, and mipmaps precompute it for minified ones. Seeing the single-texel checkerboard collapse from swirling moiré to flat gray when level sampling was switched on made that click.

Task 1: Drawing Single-Color Triangles

Algorithm

My triangle rasterizer decides, for each candidate pixel, whether the center of that pixel lies inside the triangle, and fills it if so. Sample points sit at half-integer coordinates, so pixel \((x, y)\) is tested at \((x + 0.5,\ y + 0.5)\).

The inside test uses three edge functions. For an edge from vertex \(A\) to vertex \(B\) and a sample point \(P\), \[ E_{AB}(P) = (P_x - A_x)(B_y - A_y) - (P_y - A_y)(B_x - A_x). \] This is the 2D cross product of the edge direction with the vector from \(A\) to \(P\). It is zero when \(P\) is exactly on the line through \(A\) and \(B\), and its sign tells which side of the line \(P\) is on. I evaluate the three edges going around the triangle in order, \(P_0 \to P_1\), \(P_1 \to P_2\), \(P_2 \to P_0\). A sample is inside the triangle when all three values have the same sign.

Two details fall out of this test. First, the SVG vertices may be listed clockwise or counter-clockwise, which flips the sign of every edge function. Instead of reordering vertices, I accept a sample when the three values are either all \(\geq 0\) or all \(\leq 0\), so both winding orders work with no extra work. Second, using non-strict comparisons means a sample that lies exactly on an edge counts as inside, which is what the spec asks for.

Once a sample passes, I call fill_pixel(x, y, color) to write the color into the sample buffer. At the end of the frame, resolve_to_framebuffer() converts the float colors in the sample buffer into the 8-bit RGB framebuffer.

Efficiency: only the bounding box is checked

Before looping, I compute the axis-aligned bounding box of the triangle from the min and max of the three x values and the three y values. Both corners are floored to get inclusive integer pixel indices, and both are clamped to \([0, \text{width}-1]\) and \([0, \text{height}-1]\) so triangles that hang off the screen never index outside the buffer. The double loop then visits only pixels in that box, so the work is proportional to the area of the bounding box rather than the area of the framebuffer. This is exactly the baseline the spec requires. The outer loop runs over rows and the inner loop over columns, so writes into the sample buffer are sequential in memory.

Task 2: Antialiasing by Supersampling

Algorithm and data structures

Supersampling renders the scene at a higher resolution than the screen and then averages back down. Instead of testing one point per pixel, I test an \(n \times n\) grid of points inside every pixel, where \(n = \sqrt{\text{sample\_rate}}\), and keep every result. At the end of the frame, each pixel's final color is the mean of its samples. A pixel that is fully inside a triangle averages to the triangle color, a pixel fully outside stays the background color, and a pixel on the edge blends the two in proportion to how many of its samples the triangle covered.

The only data structure is sample_buffer, a std::vector<Color> of floating-point colors that holds every sample for every pixel. Its size is width * height * sample_rate. I store all of a pixel's samples contiguously, so sample \(s\) of pixel \((x, y)\) lives at index \[ (y \cdot \text{width} + x) \cdot \text{sample\_rate} + s, \qquad s = j \cdot n + i, \] where \(i\) and \(j\) are the sub-column and sub-row inside the pixel. Keeping a pixel's samples adjacent makes the averaging step a short loop over consecutive memory.

Why supersampling helps

With one sample per pixel, a pixel is either entirely inside or entirely outside a triangle, so edges are stair-stepped and any feature thinner than a pixel can be missed entirely. Supersampling approximates the true area coverage of each pixel, which is the same as applying a box filter to the high-resolution image before downsampling. Edges become smooth gradients instead of hard steps, and thin features leave a faint trace in every pixel they touch instead of vanishing when they slip between sample points.

Changes to the rasterization pipeline

  • set_sample_rate and set_framebuffer_target now resize sample_buffer to width * height * sample_rate. Both must do this because either can be called after the other.
  • rasterize_triangle gained two inner loops over \(i, j \in [0, n)\). The sample position is \((x + \frac{i + 0.5}{n},\ y + \frac{j + 0.5}{n})\), the three edge tests from Task 1 run unchanged on that point, and a hit writes the color directly into the sample's slot in sample_buffer. At \(n = 1\) this reduces exactly to Task 1.
  • fill_pixel, used by points and lines, now writes the same color into all sample_rate slots of the pixel. Without this the black canvas border, which is drawn with rasterize_line, would be averaged against white samples and turn gray.
  • resolve_to_framebuffer sums each pixel's samples, multiplies by 1 / sample_rate, then converts each channel from a float in \([0, 1]\) to an 8-bit value in rgb_framebuffer_target.

clear_buffers did not need to change since it already fills the whole vector.

Results

The pixel inspector is over the same spot on the magenta sliver in all three. At rate 1 every pixel is either full magenta or white, and the sliver breaks into disconnected fragments where it is thinner than a pixel and no pixel center lands inside it. At rate 4 each pixel has four chances to hit the triangle, so the gaps close and the edge pixels take on intermediate pinks depending on whether one, two, or three of their samples were covered. At rate 16 the sliver is continuous and its edges fade smoothly from magenta through several shades of pink to white, because each pixel's color now tracks the fraction of its area the triangle actually covers.

Task 3: Transforms

Implementation

The three transforms are \(3 \times 3\) matrices that act on points written in homogeneous coordinates \((x, y, 1)\). The extra coordinate is what lets a matrix multiply express translation, which a plain \(2 \times 2\) matrix cannot do since it always maps the origin to itself. The starter code's operator* lifts each Vector2D to \((x, y, 1)\), multiplies, and divides by the third component to come back to 2D.

\[ T(d_x, d_y) = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 0 & d_x \\ 0 & 1 & d_y \\ 0 & 0 & 1 \end{bmatrix}, \qquad S(s_x, s_y) = \begin{bmatrix} s_x & 0 & 0 \\ 0 & s_y & 0 \\ 0 & 0 & 1 \end{bmatrix}, \qquad R(\theta) = \begin{bmatrix} \cos\theta & -\sin\theta & 0 \\ \sin\theta & \cos\theta & 0 \\ 0 & 0 & 1 \end{bmatrix} \]

rotate receives degrees, so it converts to radians before calling cos and sin. The rotation is counter-clockwise in the usual mathematical convention. Because screen and SVG coordinates have \(y\) pointing down, a positive angle appears clockwise on screen, which matches the SVG spec.

Nested <g transform="..."> groups compose by multiplying their matrices outer to inner, so the transform listed last in the nesting is applied to the geometry first. With these three functions in place, svg/transforms/robot.svg renders identically to the reference image.

My robot: a wave

I posed cubeman waving with his right arm. The original arms are rectangles centered on their own midpoints, so rotating them in place would spin each segment about its middle rather than about a joint. To get a proper joint, each segment uses the pattern translate to the pivot, rotate, then translate the segment outward along the rotated axis. The upper arm is wrapped in translate(50 -40) to reach the shoulder, then rotate(-60) to swing it up, then translate(40 0) so the rectangle extends away from the shoulder instead of straddling it. The forearm sits inside that same rotated frame: it moves out to the elbow with translate(90 0), bends a further rotate(-50), and is pushed out with translate(35 0). Because the elbow group is nested inside the shoulder group, changing only the shoulder angle moves the whole arm as a unit, which is what makes the hierarchy useful for animation. Sweeping the shoulder angle back and forth over time would produce a waving motion.

Task 4: Barycentric coordinates

What barycentric coordinates are

Barycentric coordinates describe a point \(P\) inside a triangle as a weighted average of the three vertices: \[ P = \alpha P_0 + \beta P_1 + \gamma P_2, \qquad \alpha + \beta + \gamma = 1. \] Each weight measures how close \(P\) is to one vertex. At \(P_0\) the coordinates are \((1, 0, 0)\), at the midpoint of the edge \(P_1 P_2\) they are \((0, \tfrac12, \tfrac12)\), and at the centroid they are \((\tfrac13, \tfrac13, \tfrac13)\). Inside the triangle all three weights lie in \([0, 1]\), and a point is outside exactly when one of them goes negative. Geometrically, \(\alpha\) is the area of the sub-triangle \(P P_1 P_2\) divided by the area of the whole triangle, and similarly for the others.

The reason they matter is that once you have \((\alpha, \beta, \gamma)\) for a sample, you can interpolate any per-vertex quantity with the same weights. For colors, \[ C(P) = \alpha C_0 + \beta C_1 + \gamma C_2. \] The triangle below has a red, a green, and a blue vertex. Every interior pixel is the blend given by its barycentric weights, so the color shifts smoothly from pure red at the top toward green and blue at the bottom corners, and the center is a muddy mix of all three.

Implementation

The three edge functions from Task 1 are already proportional to the barycentric coordinates. The edge function for edge \(P_1 P_2\) is zero along that edge and grows linearly toward \(P_0\), which is exactly the behavior of \(\alpha\). So inside the sampling loop, for each sample that passes the inside test I compute \[ \alpha = \frac{E_{12}}{E_{01} + E_{12} + E_{20}}, \qquad \beta = \frac{E_{20}}{E_{01} + E_{12} + E_{20}}, \qquad \gamma = \frac{E_{01}}{E_{01} + E_{12} + E_{20}}, \] where each weight uses the edge opposite its vertex. The denominator is twice the signed area of the triangle. Because the numerator and denominator flip sign together for a clockwise triangle, the ratios are correct for either winding order. The interpolated color \(\alpha C_0 + \beta C_1 + \gamma C_2\) is then written into the sample buffer at the same slot the single-color rasterizer would have used, so supersampling from Task 2 works unchanged.

Task 5: "Pixel sampling" for texture mapping

What pixel sampling is

A textured triangle stores a texture coordinate \((u, v) \in [0,1]^2\) at each vertex instead of a color. For every sample inside the triangle I interpolate \((u, v)\) with the same barycentric weights as Task 4, which gives a continuous position in the texture image. That position almost never lands exactly on a texel center, so "pixel sampling" is the rule for turning a continuous texture position into one color. I implemented two rules, selected with the P key.

Nearest scales \(u\) by the texture width and \(v\) by the height, floors both to get the texel that contains the point, clamps the indices to the image, and returns that texel unchanged. It is one memory read per sample.

Bilinear treats texel centers as sitting at half-integer positions, so it subtracts 0.5 from the scaled coordinates, floors to find the top-left texel of the surrounding \(2 \times 2\) block, and keeps the fractional parts \(s\) and \(t\) as blend weights. It reads the four neighboring texels, linearly interpolates the top pair and the bottom pair by \(s\), then interpolates those two results by \(t\). Four reads and three lerps per sample.

Implementation

sample_nearest and sample_bilinear live in texture.cpp and operate on a given MipLevel via its get_texel helper. Both clamp every texel index into the valid range so that \(u = 1\) or slight barycentric drift outside \([0,1]\) cannot read past the end of the texel array. rasterize_textured_triangle reuses the supersampled loop from Task 2, interpolates \(u\) and \(v\), fills a SampleParams struct with the coordinate and the current pixel and level sampling modes, and calls Texture::sample, which for this task dispatches to the chosen sampler at mip level 0.

Results

All four images are svg/texmap/test5.svg, the Berkeley seal warped through a spiral. The seal texture is only 260 x 260 texels stretched across the canvas, so texels are magnified on screen and the sampling method is easy to see. The pixel inspector is over the same white letter edge in every shot.

With nearest sampling at 1 sample per pixel, the edge of the letter is a hard staircase: each screen pixel copies exactly one texel, so the magnified texel grid is visible and the boundary jumps between pure white and pure blue. Supersampling to 16 softens the staircase by averaging screen samples that straddle a texel boundary, but the underlying blocks are still there because every one of those 16 samples is itself a hard nearest lookup. Bilinear at 1 sample per pixel already looks smoother than nearest at 16, because the blend happens in texture space: each pixel is a weighted mix of the four texels around it, so the transition across the letter edge is a gradient several pixels wide instead of a step. Bilinear at 16 is the smoothest of the four, though the improvement over bilinear at 1 is small here.

When the difference is large

Bilinear beats nearest most clearly when the texture is magnified, that is, when one texel covers many screen pixels. Nearest then paints each texel as a flat block and the image looks pixelated, while bilinear reconstructs a smooth surface between texel centers. It also helps when the texture is slowly moving or rotating relative to the pixel grid, since nearest lookups snap and shimmer as the boundary between two texels crosses pixel centers, whereas bilinear changes continuously. The two methods look nearly identical when the texture is displayed close to its native resolution, and neither fixes the opposite problem of minification, where many texels fall inside one pixel. Both only look at one or four texels per sample, so a minified high-frequency texture still aliases. That case is what mipmapping in Task 6 addresses. The cost of bilinear is four texel reads and three interpolations instead of one read, which is a noticeable but not dominant slowdown on a software rasterizer.

Task 6: "Level Sampling" with mipmaps for texture mapping

What level sampling is

Pixel sampling from Task 5 reads at most four texels per sample no matter how large the texture is. When a texture is minified, meaning many texels project into a single screen pixel, those four texels are an arbitrary subset of what the pixel actually covers, so the result sparkles and forms moiré patterns. A mipmap fixes this by precomputing a pyramid of downsampled copies of the texture: level 0 is full resolution, level 1 is half the size in each dimension, level 2 a quarter, and so on, each one a box-filtered version of the one before. Level sampling is the rule for choosing which level to read from, so that one texel at the chosen level covers roughly one screen pixel. The L key cycles the three modes.

The right level depends on how fast the texture coordinate changes across the screen. If stepping one pixel to the right moves \((u, v)\) by \(2^k\) texels, then level \(k\) is the one whose texels have been pre-averaged over that footprint. So \[ D = \log_2 \max\!\left( \left\| \frac{\partial (u,v)}{\partial x} \right\|,\ \left\| \frac{\partial (u,v)}{\partial y} \right\| \right), \] with the derivatives measured in texels, that is, scaled by the full-resolution texture width and height. \(D\) is a continuous number and can be negative when the texture is magnified; negative values clamp to level 0 since there is nothing finer.

Implementation

In rasterize_textured_triangle, for each covered sample I compute barycentric coordinates not only at the sample point but also at the points one full pixel to the right and one full pixel down, interpolate \((u, v)\) at all three, and store them in the SampleParams fields p_uv, p_dx_uv, and p_dy_uv. The neighbor points are not tested for triangle coverage, since only the rate of change matters.

Texture::get_level subtracts p_uv from each neighbor to get the two difference vectors, scales their \(x\) components by the texture width and \(y\) components by the height, takes the larger of the two lengths, and returns its \(\log_2\), or 0 if that would be negative or undefined.

Texture::sample then branches on the level mode. L_ZERO always uses level 0, as in Task 5. L_NEAREST rounds \(D\) to the nearest integer, clamps it to the valid range of mip levels, and passes it to the pixel sampler chosen by psm. L_LINEAR clamps \(D\) as a float, floors it to get the lower level, takes the next level up as the upper, samples both with the chosen pixel sampler, and blends them by the fractional part of \(D\). With P_LINEAR this is trilinear filtering. The pixel and level choices are independent, so all six combinations work.

Tradeoffs

  • Speed. Per sample, nearest pixel costs 1 texel read and bilinear costs 4. Level sampling adds the get_level computation, which is two extra barycentric evaluations and a logarithm, plus nothing more for L_NEAREST but a second full pixel sample for L_LINEAR. Trilinear is therefore 8 reads and 7 lerps per sample, roughly 8 times the memory traffic of the cheapest option. In practice the mip levels are small and cache well, so minified textures can actually render faster with mipmapping than without, because level 0 reads scattered across a large image are replaced by reads that stay within a small one.
  • Memory. A mipmap stores the full texture plus levels of \(\tfrac14, \tfrac1{16}, \ldots\) of its size, which sums to \(\tfrac43\) of the original. The extra third is paid once at load time, not per frame.
  • Antialiasing power. Pixel sampling only helps with magnification. Supersampling helps with everything but at \(N\) times the cost for \(N\) samples. Level sampling targets minification directly: by reading a level whose texels already average the pixel's footprint, one sample at the right level does what many supersamples would do, for close to constant cost. L_NEAREST can show a visible seam where the rounded level changes; L_LINEAR blends across that boundary at the cost of the second sample.

Results

To make the effect obvious I generated a 1024 x 1024 test texture, img/pattern.png, whose left half is a checkerboard of single texels with a coarse red grid every 128 texels, and whose right half is a set of concentric rings and radial spokes with a two-texel period. It is mapped through the same warped mesh as texmap/test6.svg. At the default view the texture is minified by roughly two to four times over most of the surface, and magnified in a small region near the lower left. All four images are at 1 sample per pixel so that only the texture filtering changes.

At level zero the single-texel checkerboard is far above the pixel sampling rate, so both pixel modes produce swirling moiré: nearest picks essentially random texels and bilinear only averages four of the many texels under each pixel, which is not enough. Switching to nearest level selects mip level 1 or 2 across most of the surface, where the checkerboard has already been box-filtered to a uniform gray. The moiré vanishes and the red grid, which is wide enough to survive several levels of downsampling, stays sharp. In the L_NEAREST, P_NEAREST image a patch in the lower left remains noisy. That is the one region where the mesh magnifies the texture, so \(D\) rounds to 0 and level 0 is correctly chosen, and a nearest lookup into a single-texel checkerboard is still hit-or-miss. In the L_NEAREST, P_LINEAR image the same patch is smooth, because bilinear filtering handles the magnified case that mipmapping does not. The two techniques are complementary: level sampling fixes minification, pixel sampling fixes magnification, and only the combination is clean everywhere. Bilinear alone at level zero, in the top right image, softens the moiré but leaves large ring-shaped artifacts, since four texels are still far fewer than the pixel's true footprint.
